**In Short**

- Healthcare advertising fails on compliance far more often than on budget.
- Ad platforms restrict health targeting, so the creative and the landing page
  have to do the targeting work instead.
- A patient enquiry is a conversation, not a form submission — response time is
  part of ad performance.
- You cannot pass patient data back to ad platforms, so measurement has to be
  designed differently from e-commerce.
- Cost per enquiry is a vanity metric on its own; cost per *qualified* enquiry
  is the number that matters.

Most healthcare brands do not have an advertising problem. They have a
translation problem: they run ads built for e-commerce logic in a category where
e-commerce logic is not allowed to work. The targeting options are narrower, the
creative review is stricter, the conversion is slower, and the data you are
permitted to collect is smaller. Advertising in healthcare works — but only when
the campaign is designed around those constraints instead of against them.

## Why Healthcare Advertising Is Not Like Other Advertising

Three constraints shape every healthcare campaign.

**Targeting is restricted.** Major ad platforms treat health status as a
sensitive category. Google's personalized advertising policy lists physical and
mental health conditions among the categories advertisers
[may not use to build audiences](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465),
and Meta applies comparable restrictions under its
[Advertising Standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/).
In practice this means you generally cannot target a condition, a diagnosis, or a
treatment someone is seeking. This is the single most common reason a campaign
that "worked in another sector" collapses in healthcare — the audience-building
step that carried the whole strategy is simply unavailable. Policies differ by
platform and change over time, so check the current policy text before planning a
campaign around any targeting assumption.

**Creative is reviewed against health policy.** Google's
[healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)
and Meta's Advertising Standards both set additional requirements for health
advertising, and in our experience the common rejection triggers are
before-and-after imagery, outcome claims, implied guarantees and language that
implies personal knowledge of the viewer's health. A rejected ad is not just a
delay; repeated rejections can affect account standing. What is permitted also
varies by country, so the same creative may clear review in one market and fail
in another.

**The decision is slow and human.** Nobody books a medical procedure the way
they buy a pair of shoes. The gap between first impression and first enquiry is
measured in weeks, and it is filled with reading, comparing and asking other
people.

Each of these constraints moves work away from the targeting panel and onto the
things you fully control: the creative, the landing page, and what happens after
someone gets in touch.

## Let the Creative Do the Targeting

When you cannot target the condition, the ad itself has to find the right person
and — just as importantly — let the wrong person scroll past.

That means being specific about the *situation* rather than the *diagnosis*.
Naming the treatment, the location, the language you serve patients in and the
practical questions people actually ask does more filtering than any audience
setting would have. Vague, reassuring "your health matters to us" creative is
the worst of both worlds: it passes policy review and attracts nobody.

Two things we keep coming back to in practice:

**Show the clinic, not stock photography.** Real rooms, real equipment, real
staff. Healthcare audiences are unusually good at spotting generic imagery, and
the credibility cost is immediate.

**Answer the objection in the ad.** Cost, recovery time, whether an interpreter
is available, whether the first consultation is remote. Putting the friction in
the ad reduces unqualified enquiries, which is the metric that actually decides
whether a campaign is profitable.

## The Landing Page Is Part of the Ad

In healthcare the landing page carries a load it does not carry elsewhere,
because it has to establish trust that the platform will not let you assume.

Practitioner credentials, accreditations, the physical address, and named staff
belong above the fold, not on a separate About page. Patient reviews matter, but
only real, attributable ones — in a category this sensitive, unverifiable praise
reads as a warning sign rather than reassurance.

Give people more than one way to make contact. Some will fill in a form. Many
more — particularly international patients — will only ever use WhatsApp, and
they will do it at hours that have nothing to do with your working day.

And keep the page in the language of the ad. A campaign in German that lands on
an English page loses most of what the ad achieved. Machine-translated medical
content loses more than that: it damages the credibility the ad just paid for.

## Multi-Market Campaigns: What We Learned on Dentasay

For Dentasay we brought the website, social media, digital advertising, content
production and international patient communication together under one strategy,
across several countries and languages — the full scope is set out in our
[Dentasay case study](/en/projects/dentasay/).

The practical lesson was that "the same campaign, translated" does not work.
Patients arriving from different markets ask different first questions and have
different expectations about how quickly a clinic replies. The parts that had to
be rebuilt per market were the creative angle, the landing content and the
enquiry handling — not the media plan.

The second lesson was that advertising and patient communication cannot be run
by separate teams on separate timelines. Ad spend converts at the speed the
clinic answers messages. When the reply is fast and in the patient's own
language, the same budget produces a different result.

## Measuring Without Crossing the Privacy Line

This is where most healthcare accounts quietly break.

Ad platform terms and data-protection law both restrict sending
patient-identifying data or health-related detail back to an ad platform, and the
exact obligations depend on where your patients are. That rules out the standard
conversion setup a lot of agencies apply by default, and it means the measurement
design has to be deliberate:

**Count the enquiry, not the condition.** Track that a contact happened. Do not
pass what it was about.

**Track WhatsApp properly.** If a large share of enquiries arrive on WhatsApp
and only form submissions are counted, the reported cost per lead is wrong —
usually wrong enough to shut down the campaign that was actually working.

**Add a qualification step.** Not every enquiry is a potential patient. Without
someone marking which enquiries were real, optimisation targets volume, and
volume in healthcare is cheap and worthless.

**Accept the reporting gap.** Long consideration windows plus restricted
tracking means some conversions will never be attributable. Building the
strategy around a number that will always be incomplete leads to worse decisions
than accepting the gap and reading the trend.

## A Realistic Starting Structure

For a clinic or health brand beginning paid media, we would usually start
narrow:

- **One treatment, one market, one language.** Enough budget to produce
  interpretable data instead of noise across five campaigns.
- **Search before social.** Search catches people already looking; it tells you
  what they call the treatment, which then informs everything else.
- **A dedicated landing page**, not the homepage.
- **Enquiry handling agreed before launch** — who replies, in which language,
  within how long.
- **A qualification review after the first weeks**, before scaling anything.

Expanding to more markets is straightforward once one market is understood.
Running five markets badly at once teaches you nothing about any of them.

## Conclusion

Healthcare digital advertising rewards discipline more than budget. The
constraints — restricted targeting, strict creative review, slow decisions,
limited data — are not obstacles to work around; they are the design brief. The
brands that do well are the ones that move the effort into the creative, the
landing page and the response process, and that measure qualified enquiries
rather than impressions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you target people by medical condition in digital advertising?

Generally no. Major ad platforms classify health status as a sensitive category
and restrict targeting built around conditions, diagnoses or treatments being
sought — see Google's
[restricted targeting policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465)
and Meta's [Advertising Standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/).
Healthcare campaigns have to do their filtering through the creative and the
landing page instead. Policy details vary by platform and by country, so confirm
the current rules for your markets.

### Why do healthcare ads get rejected so often?

In our experience the most common triggers are outcome claims and implied
guarantees, before-and-after imagery, and copy that implies knowledge of the
viewer's personal health situation. Requirements differ between platforms and
markets, so reviewing creative against the platform's current health policy
before submission is faster than appealing afterwards.

### Which platform works best for healthcare advertising?

It depends on where the patient is in their decision. Search reaches people
already looking for a treatment and usually produces higher-intent enquiries.
Social builds awareness and works well for visual treatment categories, but
enquiries take longer to mature. Most healthcare brands need both, starting with
search.

### How should healthcare ad performance be measured?

By qualified patient enquiries, not impressions, clicks or raw lead count. That
requires tracking every enquiry channel — including WhatsApp and phone — and a
step where someone marks which enquiries were genuine potential patients.

### How long before healthcare advertising produces results?

Longer than in most sectors, because the decision is slow. Expect the first
interpretable data in weeks rather than days, and expect the earliest enquiries
to be research-stage rather than ready to book.
